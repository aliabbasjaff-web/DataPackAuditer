# Basis — the complete walkthrough & defense prep

*Study doc for the Mercor Enterprise AI technical screen. Everything here is true of the
actual repo — nothing is embellished, which is the whole point: you can be probed on any
of it and it holds.*

---

## 1. The story in 90 seconds

> "AI agents can now generate banker-grade work product — memos, IC datapacks, models.
> I've watched what it takes for a document to be trusted inside a bank, and the AI
> versions have no equivalent of the person who checks the model before it goes to the MD.
> So I built that checker. Basis takes an AI-generated financial document — a drafted
> brief or a generated Excel pack — and audits every number in it against what the company
> actually filed with the SEC. Verified numbers keep a citation; wrong ones get flagged;
> untraceable ones get stripped rather than trusted.
>
> The results made the case better than I expected. The same model drafting from memory
> got 10% of its claims past the audit — and the errors weren't random, they were *stale*:
> last year's true numbers asserted as current. Drafting against source data: 87.5%, and
> the only failures were the gate refusing what it couldn't check. Then I pointed the
> auditor at an Excel datapack generated by Anthropic's own public investment-banking
> agent — which promises 'zero tolerance for errors' in its instructions, with nothing
> enforcing it — and it verified 128 of 139 cells and caught one real defect: a fake 0.0%
> growth figure manufactured by a link to an empty cell.
>
> The most useful output is the failure log — ten specific ways the agent, the
> environment, or my own auditor went wrong, with root causes. Happy to go through any
> of them."

Stop there. Let them pick the thread. Every thread leads somewhere you're prepared.

## 2. Why Super Micro (you will be asked, and the answer is a flex)

Four reasons, in the order to give them:

1. **I know the name.** UBS ran Super Micro's $633M follow-on; it's on my deal list. I
   can tell whether the tool's output is *analytically* right, not just numerically
   right — and picking a company where I can judge output quality is the same judgment
   call Mercor makes hiring domain experts to write evals.
2. **It's the perfect trust story.** SMCI's whole 2024 *was* a number-trust crisis: a
   short-seller report, a delayed 10-K, EY resigning as auditor, an NT 10-K, near
   delisting. Demoing a "can you trust the numbers" tool on the company where the market
   literally couldn't is not an accident.
3. **The financials stress-test the verifier.** Revenue +110% one year, +47% the next;
   gross margin compressing 270bps; operating cash flow swinging from −$2.5B to +$1.7B;
   inventory tripling in a year. Extreme, fast-moving numbers are exactly where a model's
   stale memory fails loudest — which made the ungrounded-vs-grounded contrast sharp. A
   sleepy utility would have hidden the problem.
4. **The fiscal year ends June 30.** Deliberately awkward: "FY2024" ≠ calendar 2024,
   which is a classic trap for both models and naive code, and I wanted period-alignment
   to be a real problem, not a toy one.

## 3. System map

```
             (open network)                        (anywhere — incl. locked-down envs)
  SEC EDGAR XBRL API ──► edgar.py ──► data/cache/smci.json ─┬─► verify.py ────► verdicts ─► render.py ─► HTML report
   "the answer key"      fetch layer      the cache boundary │   audits BRIEFS
                                                             └─► xlsx_audit.py ► verdicts
                                                                 audits WORKBOOKS
  LLM (Claude) sits ONLY here:  drafting briefs, building the datapack.
  Never in the data path. Never doing the checking.
```

The one-sentence architecture: **AI at the edges, deterministic code in the middle, and a
cache boundary so the trusted core never needs the network.**

## 4. File by file — what it is, why it's that way, what to say

### `data/cache/smci.json` — the answer key
Nine XBRL concepts (revenue, gross profit, operating income, net income, inventory,
OCF, cash, R&D, AR) for FY2019–FY2025, pulled from EDGAR's companyconcept API, plus the
10-K accession numbers they came from.

**Decisions to defend:**
- *Why a cache at all?* Because the build environment's egress proxy blocked sec.gov
  (Failure #1). Enterprise networks do exactly this. Splitting fetch from verify means
  the trusted core runs offline — better architecture, forced by the constraint.
- *Why only 9 concepts?* Deliberate scope: every concept in the cache needs a
  period-alignment rule and a mapping entry in the verifier. The list is a versioned
  "evidence base" — when a claim needs a concept that isn't there (capex, for the FCF
  claim), the gate strips the claim, and the fix is an explicit, reviewable addition.
  This is Failure #5, and it's the "why doesn't the report mention X" client
  conversation in miniature.

### `basis/edgar.py` — the fetch layer
Live mode (direct HTTP, works on your laptop) and cache mode (reads the snapshot). The
`annual_series` function collapses raw XBRL into `{period: value}` keeping only 10-K
facts, and where a period appears in multiple filings it keeps the **most recent
filing's value** — because each 10-K restates two prior years, and you want restated
numbers, not originals.

**Decisions to defend:**
- *Why keep-latest?* Restatements are corrections; the latest filing is the company's
  current official position. Bonus: the overlap (most values appear in 2–3 filings) is a
  free consistency check on the data itself.
- *Why the SEC rate-limit sleep?* EDGAR allows ~10 req/s; being a polite client is part
  of building on public infrastructure.
- Know this term: **XBRL** — the machine-readable tagging standard for SEC filings.
  Failure #6 lives here: "revenue" isn't one tag. SMCI's revenue is under
  `RevenueFromContractWithCustomerExcludingAssessedTax` (the post-ASC-606 tag), and the
  plain `Revenues` tag is nearly empty. XBRL is a *vocabulary, not a schema* — filers
  choose tags, so cross-company scaling needs a mapping layer. That's real enterprise
  data work: standardized container, heterogeneous semantics.

### `drafts/*.json` — the claim schema (the most important design idea in the repo)
The drafting AI doesn't produce prose; it produces **typed claims**. Three kinds:
- `fact`: a figure + a source ref (concept + period). "Revenue was $21.97B" must say
  *which* filed number it's asserting.
- `derived`: a figure + a derivation spec (`yoy_growth`, `margin`, `ratio`, `delta` over
  named primitives). "Grew 46.6%" must say *how to recompute it*.
- `qualitative`: no number to check ("EY resigned as auditor"). Labeled for human
  review — the gate neither blesses nor strips what it can't check.

**Why this matters — say it like this:** "The schema converts *generation* into
*auditable assertion*. Free text is uncheckable; a typed claim is a contract. This is
the same idea as Mercor's prompt–rubric pairs: capture the judgment in a structure a
machine can apply at scale."

**The trade you accepted:** the derivation spec is a tiny language, and any language
draws a line — DSO (AR/revenue×365) fell outside it because there's no scalar multiply
(Failure #4). The gate stripped a *true* claim. That's the right default: the promise
is "nothing unchecked gets through," not "everything true gets through."

### `basis/verify.py` — the citation gate for briefs (~150 lines)
For each claim: look up the cited fact in the cache → compare (facts) or recompute from
cited primitives and compare (derived) → verdict: `VERIFIED / MISMATCH / UNSOURCED /
UNVERIFIABLE`. Outputs a tally and a **grounding rate** (share of quantitative claims
verified) — the project's headline metric.

**Decisions to defend:**
- *Why is MISMATCH worse than UNSOURCED?* A mismatched claim *cites* a source and
  contradicts it — it looks grounded. Unsourced at least announces its own weakness.
- *Tolerances (Failure #7):* 0.5% **relative** on dollar figures (permits "$22.0B" as
  prose rounding of 21,972; catches stale numbers), 0.4pp **absolute** on percentages
  (relative tolerance on an 11% margin would be 0.06pp — nothing human-written would
  ever pass). One rule can't serve both jobs. And say the kicker: *tolerance is a
  per-use-case product decision — a pitch deck absorbs 0.6%, a covenant calculation
  absorbs zero.*
- *Why deterministic, not an LLM judge?* See probe Q3 below — this is the question you
  most want them to ask.

### `agent_run/build_datapack.py` — the agent-under-test
Builds the SMCI Excel datapack by following Anthropic's public `datapack-builder` skill
**verbatim**: formulas for every calculation (their RULE 6), blue-input/black-formula/
green-link color coding, $M units declared in headers, accounting negatives, source
citations per row, cross-tab links, 94 formulas, clean recalculation.

**The one-pass rule:** built once, per the instructions, *not* hand-tuned after seeing
audit results. Otherwise the experiment is rigged. The audit found what it found.

**The setup line for interviews:** "Anthropic's plugin instructions say 'zero tolerance
for errors — trace every number to source.' That's a promise written in prose, with no
mechanism. I built the mechanism, then tested their agent against their own standard."

### `basis/xlsx_audit.py` — the workbook auditor (the technically deepest file)
Treats the generated workbook as an untrusted claim set. Five checks:
1. **Input cells** (hardcoded numbers): map the row label to an XBRL concept
   (regex-based mapping table — the judgment layer), detect the sheet's scale from the
   header ("$ in millions" → ×1e6), compare to the filed value.
2. **Formula cells**: take the *recalculated value* and recompute the metric
   independently from the cache. The formula being well-formed proves nothing; only
   recompute-and-compare catches a right-looking wrong cell.
3. **RULE-6 enforcement**: rows whose labels mark them as calculations ("margin",
   "growth", "%", "total") must contain formulas, not hardcodes — the plugin's own rule,
   now enforced.
4. **Identity checks**: COGS + gross profit must equal revenue, etc.
5. **Narrative scan**: numbers living inside prose cells ("$5.2B", "270bps") are
   extracted and flagged `NARRATIVE_UNAUDITED` — prose is an audit bypass (Failure #10).

**Mechanics worth knowing cold (real openpyxl gotcha):** reading a workbook takes TWO
loads — one gives you formula strings with no values, one gives you cached values with
no formulas. And a freshly written workbook has *no* cached values until something
(LibreOffice headless, here) recalculates it. If an engineer asks "how do you read the
formulas AND the values," this is the answer, and knowing it signals you actually
touched the problem.

**The v1→v2 story (Failure #8) — your best "iteration" answer:** v1 had both classic
checker failure modes at once. It *over-flagged* (the narrative scanner matched any
digit-string, so 11 of its 15 flags were the workbook's own source citations — accession
numbers parsed as claims) and it *under-checked* (all 94 formula cells fell through
every branch and produced no finding — silence that read as a pass). v2 added the
derived-metric recompute table and restricted the prose scanner to money/percent/bps
patterns. Punchline: **"an audit's silence must be distinguishable from its approval"**
and **"checkers need checking — an eval that overflags gets ignored, one that
underflags launders errors."**

**The catch (Failure #9) — your best single finding:** Executive Summary B9 shows
"0.0%" revenue growth for FY2021. The source tab deliberately leaves that cell blank
(no FY2020 column to compute from). But the summary *links* to it, and Excel evaluates
a reference to an empty cell as numeric zero — so a deliberate "n/a" became a
confident, wrong number one tab away. The agent followed every formatting rule and the
rules manufactured the error. Invisible to formula inspection, invisible to
recalculation (no error value), caught only by recompute-and-compare. This is the
finding to lead with when asked "what did it catch?"

### `basis/render.py` — the report
Single-file HTML: scoreband (10% vs 87.5%), the grounded brief with per-claim badges,
the ungrounded "before," full audit trail, and the method note (tolerances disclosed —
an audit rule nobody can see isn't an audit rule).

### `FAILURES.md` — the actual deliverable
Ten failures, each as symptom → root cause → fix → generalization. In the interview,
don't recite it — *deploy* it: whatever system topic comes up, you have a first-hand
failure for it. Networks → #1. Hallucination → #2 or #3. Evals → #8. Excel/tooling →
#9. Scope/product → #4, #5. Data engineering → #6. Tolerance/metrics → #7.

## 5. The design decisions you must own (the AI didn't make these)

1. **Trust is the product, not generation.** Everyone builds generators; the gap is the
   audit layer. (Problem selection — the most senior-sounding thing you can defend.)
2. **Strip, don't trust.** Unverifiable claims are removed, not kept with a warning.
   Default chosen for financial workflows specifically.
3. **The checker is deterministic.** No LLM grades the output. Non-negotiable core.
4. **AI never sits in the data path** — drafting only. Adopted after Failure #2, when a
   model-mediated fetch fabricated a filing record.
5. **Typed claims over free text.** Auditability designed in at the schema level.
6. **Split tolerances**, disclosed in the report, per-use-case in principle.
7. **A versioned evidence base** (`CORE_CONCEPTS`) instead of "fetch whatever."
8. **No framework, no vector DB, no fine-tune.** ~300 lines of stdlib Python +
   openpyxl. For structured financial data, exact lookup beats embeddings; the
   complexity budget went to the verifier, not the stack.

## 6. Probe questions and strong answers

**Q1. "How do you know the output is correct?"**
That's the whole project — the audit is the answer. Every number traces to a filed
value or a recomputation from filed values; the grounding rate quantifies it; the audit
trail in the report shows every check. And where the system *can't* know (qualitative
claims, prose numbers), it says so explicitly instead of pretending.

**Q2. "How do you know the *auditor* is correct?"** *(the good interviewers ask this)*
Three honest layers: (a) the cache cross-checks itself — every 10-K restates two prior
years, so most values appear in 2–3 filings and agreed everywhere; (b) I seeded known
errors — the ungrounded draft is effectively a test suite of realistic wrong answers,
and the auditor caught 8 of 8 real mistakes plus flagged the 2 boundary cases; (c) it
already failed once and I caught it — v1 over-flagged citations and silently skipped
formulas (Failure #8), which is exactly why "checkers need checking" is in the log. What
I'd add next: a golden-test suite of deliberately corrupted workbooks.

**Q3. "Why not use an LLM as the judge/verifier?"**
Because the failure I'm defending against is fluent, confident, plausible wrongness —
and an LLM judge fails in exactly that direction. For numeric claims you don't need
judgment, you need arithmetic; a deterministic checker is cheaper, perfectly
repeatable, and can't be sweet-talked by well-written prose. Where I *did* put a model
in the data path once, it fabricated a filing record (Failure #2). LLM-as-judge earns
its place where criteria are genuinely qualitative — which is why Mercor's rubric
design matters: tight binary criteria are what make even model-based grading reliable.

**Q4. "How does this scale beyond one company?"**
The verifier and audit logic are company-agnostic already; two things aren't. The tag
mapping (Failure #6) needs a resolver — an ordered candidate list per logical metric,
pick the tag with recent coverage, *record the choice in the audit trail*. And the
label mapping in the Excel auditor needs to grow from regexes toward a curated synonym
table — which, notably, is expert-labeling work: you'd source people who know what
"turnover" means in a UK filing. At 100 companies the binding constraint is neither
compute nor model quality; it's maintaining the mapping layer — the unglamorous
judgment-heavy glue that every enterprise data deployment underestimates.

**Q5. "What's the biggest limitation?"**
Coverage. The gate only audits what it can express: nine concepts, four derivation ops,
cell-level numbers. DSO fell outside the DSL on day one; prose narrative bypasses cell
audit entirely (Failure #10). I chose to fail closed — strip what I can't check — so
the limitation costs completeness, never correctness. The roadmap is widening coverage
deliberately: each new op or concept is new verifier surface, and a wrong verifier is
worse than none because it launders errors as "verified."

**Q6. "What did it actually catch? Give me one concrete thing."**
The 0.0% growth cell (Failure #9, full story above). Second favorite: the model
asserting SMCI's *previous* year's cash balance ($1.7B) as current — off by 67% —
because stale numbers were once true and survive a smell test.

**Q7. "How much of the code did AI write?"**
Most of it — I used Claude Code as the build agent, which was half the point: the prep
advice I got was 'build something end-to-end with an agent and study where it fails.'
What I owned: the problem selection, the claim schema, strip-vs-trust, deterministic
checking, the tolerances, the scope of the evidence base, and the failure analysis. I
can walk through any file. The honest division of labor — AI wrote the code, I wrote
the definition of correct — is, as far as I can tell, the job description.

**Q8. "What would you do differently / what's next?"**
Three concrete: generate narrative *from* verified cells (templated) so prose inherits
verification instead of restating it; golden-test the auditor with deliberately
corrupted workbooks; extend the same gate to private-company data via SEC Form D for
angel screening — same architecture, messier data, which is the point.

**Q9. "This seems simple. Where's the hard part?"**
Correct — and deliberately. The hard part isn't any component; it's the *boundaries*:
what's in the evidence base, what the DSL can express, what tolerance means per use
case, where model output is allowed to touch data. Every failure in my log happened at
a boundary, not inside a component. Enterprise AI work is mostly boundary-drawing;
the components are commodities now.

**Q10. "How does this connect to what we do at Mercor?"**
Directly. Mercor's product is capturing expert judgment in machine-applicable form —
prompt–rubric pairs, evals, agents trained on how experts actually work. Basis is that
pattern applied to one banking workflow: the claim schema is the rubric, the verifier
applies it at machine scale, and the expert judgment lives in exactly the places a
model can't supply it — what counts as correct, what tolerance the use case absorbs,
what gets stripped. Building it also taught me the deployment-side lessons (egress
policy, data-path discipline, checker calibration) that I'd expect to hit in every
enterprise engagement.

## 7. Vocabulary — one plain sentence each, so you can *say* them

- **Deterministic**: same input, same output, every time — code, not judgment.
- **XBRL**: the machine-readable tagging standard for SEC filings; a vocabulary,
  not a schema — filers pick their tags.
- **Grounding rate**: share of quantitative claims that survived the audit; my
  headline metric (10% → 87.5%).
- **Claim schema / DSL**: the small structured language claims are written in; inside
  it, machine-checkable — outside it, stripped.
- **Cache boundary**: the line between "needs the network" and "runs anywhere";
  everything trusted sits on the offline side.
- **Two-pass read**: openpyxl gives you formulas or values, never both in one load.
- **Egress allowlist**: deny-by-default outbound network policy; the reason
  enterprise environments break agents written for the open internet.
- **Fail closed**: when unsure, strip — the system errs toward saying less, never
  toward being wrong.
